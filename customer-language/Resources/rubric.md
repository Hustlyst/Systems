# Customer Language rubric

This file sets how customer words are collected, sorted, counted and used. The skill reads it on every run, so the bank stays consistent from one quarter to the next.

## The six groups

Every useful quote goes into exactly one group.

| Group | What belongs here | Example of a strong quote |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Trigger** | The moment or event that made them start looking | "We lost two patients in one week because of double bookings." |
| **Pain** | The problem, in their words, ideally with what it cost them | "The front desk spends the first hour every day calling to confirm." |
| **Result** | What they wanted to have or feel afterwards | "I just want to open the calendar and trust it." |
| **Worry** | What nearly stopped them buying | "I was scared we'd lose all our history moving over." |
| **Words** | What they call the problem, the job and the category | "no-shows", "front desk", "the booking mess" |
| **Alternatives** | What else they tried, considered or kept doing | "We tried a shared spreadsheet for a year." |

A quote that fits none of the six groups is left out. Praise with no detail, such as "great product", tells a buyer nothing.

## Which sources count most

Not all customer words are equally useful for marketing. Weight them in this order.

1. **Words from before they bought:** sales call transcripts, demo questions, trial sign-up answers. These are the words of someone still deciding, which is who the copy is for.
2. **Words about the switch:** onboarding calls, "why did you choose us" survey answers, and the reasons lost deals gave.
3. **Reviews,** your own and your competitors'. Competitors' reviews show the pains your category leaves unsolved.
4. **Support tickets and community posts.** These are useful for worries and words, and weaker for triggers.

Words from your own team, such as notes that summarise what a customer meant, are never quotes.

## What counts as a pattern

A phrase or idea becomes a **pattern** when it appears in at least **three separate sources**, from at least **two kinds** of source, for example a review and a sales call. One source saying something vividly is a lead to follow up. It is not a pattern.

For each pattern, keep:

- The number of separate sources.
- The kinds of source.
- The two or three clearest quotes, word for word, with their labels.
- The date it was first and last seen, so a fading pattern is visible.

## Scoring your current copy

Each line of the copy being audited gets one mark.

| Mark | Meaning |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Backed** | It says something a pattern supports, in words close to the customers' own |
| **Backed, wrong words** | The idea is supported, but it uses your language where customers use different words |
| **Unbacked** | No pattern supports it. It may be true, but it is your opinion until tested |
| **Jargon** | It uses words no customer used, such as "platform", "seamless" or "solution" |

The **copy score** is the share of lines marked Backed. Report it as a number and keep it in `MEMORY.md`, so each rewrite can be compared with the last.

## Rules for rewriting

- The headline must use a phrase from a pattern with at least three sources.
- Every new line shows the quotes that support it, underneath, in the working draft. They come out of the final copy.
- Keep the length the same as the original unless asked.
- Never put a customer's quote in published copy as a testimonial without their permission. The bank is for learning their words. Publishing a named quote is a separate decision with its own consent.

## What the learning step looks for

Each logged copy test records which group the winning version led with: trigger, pain, result, worry or alternatives. Over several tests, the skill reports which kind of opening wins with this audience, for example "pain-led headlines beat result-led ones in 4 of 5 tests". It then leads with that group in future drafts and says so.
