# Value Pricing rubric

This file sets how a project's value is worked out, how options are priced, and how results are judged over time. The skill reads it on every proposal, so prices stay consistent from one client to the next.

## Three numbers before any price

1. **The cost of the problem today**, per year, in the client's own figures.
2. **The value of solving it**, per year. Money gained, money saved or risk avoided.
3. **The cost of their alternatives**: hiring someone, another supplier, building it themselves, or doing nothing for a year.

Each number comes from what the client said. When a needed figure is missing, it becomes an **assumption**.

## Rules for assumptions

Every assumption has:

- A **low** and a **high** estimate.
- Where the estimate came from, for example "typical for online furniture shops" or "the client said roughly".
- **The question that confirms it**, in words the reader would say to the client.

Use the **low** estimate for pricing until the client confirms. A price built on the high estimate falls apart the first time the client checks the maths.

## Kinds of value

| Kind | How it is counted | Example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Money gained | Extra revenue per year, times the share the project is responsible for | Recovered checkout orders |
| Money saved | Hours saved times the cost of those hours, or costs removed | A team no longer re-keying invoices |
| Risk avoided | The cost of the bad event times how likely it is in a year | A compliance fine, a lost key customer |
| Speed | The value of getting a result sooner, such as an earlier launch | Three months of revenue brought forward |

Count each piece of value once. When two kinds overlap, keep the one with the stronger evidence.

## Building the three options

| Option | What it includes | Usual price as a share of first-year value |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1. The core | Solves the main problem | 10 to 15 percent |
| 2. Built to last | The core, plus what keeps the result working: training, documentation, a check-in | 15 to 20 percent |
| 3. The full result | Option 2, plus the highest-value extra the client mentioned | 20 to 25 percent |

The shares are starting points. Calibration replaces them with the shares the reader actually wins at.

Every option also has:

- **What is not included**, as a short list. This is what protects the project from scope creep.
- **The timeline.**
- **One line on the result the client gets**, in their words.

## The floor

The floor is the lowest price at which the work is worth doing for the reader: their own time at their target rate, plus any subcontractors and tools, plus a margin for risk. It is never shown to the client. No option is ever priced below it. If the value cannot support a price above the floor, the right answer is a smaller scope or a polite no.

## Checks before a proposal goes out

- Redo one calculation by hand. AI makes arithmetic mistakes, and one wrong zero ruins a proposal.
- Option 2 should sit within a sensible range of past projects of similar size. A big jump is fine when the value is real, but check it.
- Every assumption that changes a price must be confirmed with the client first, or shown as an assumption in the proposal.

## What the learning step looks for

Each logged proposal records the option chosen, or "lost", and the price as a share of first-year value. Once the project ends, it also records whether the client got the result. Over time the skill reports:

- **Win rate per option.** If option 3 is never chosen, it may be too far from option 2.
- **The share of value the reader actually wins at.** This replaces the starting shares above.
- **Where the value was overstated.** If clients often get less than the estimate, the low estimates were not low enough.
